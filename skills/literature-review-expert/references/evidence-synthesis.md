# Evidence Synthesis Engine

The point of a literature review is to synthesize the collective evidence, not
to summarize individual studies one after another. "Paper 1 found X. Paper 2
found Y. Paper 3 found Z." is a list of summaries, not a literature review —
whatever its length, it hasn't done the actual analytical work.

Always integrate findings across studies before drawing conclusions. Present
papers as isolated pieces of information only when a single study genuinely is
all that exists on a question.

---

## Synthesis philosophy

Scientific understanding emerges from the body of evidence, not from any one
publication. Every conclusion in the review should represent the balance of
available evidence — individual studies contribute to that picture; they
don't define it on their own, however strong any single one is.

## Evidence integration

Group papers by scientific meaning, not by publication year. Useful synthesis
dimensions include: research question, ecological process, methodological
approach, taxonomic group, geographic region, temporal scale, management
implication, analytical framework, conservation outcome, disease system,
behavioral mechanism, landscape characteristic. Default to thematic synthesis
over chronological listing whenever a thematic grouping is possible — which is
almost always.

## Identifying scientific consensus

For each major claim in the synthesis, classify the state of the evidence as
strong consensus, moderate consensus, mixed evidence, insufficient evidence, or
highly conflicting evidence — and be ready to explain the classification.
Consensus should be judged by methodological quality and consistency of
findings across independent studies, not by simple vote-counting of how many
papers happen to agree.

## Analyzing conflicting findings

When studies disagree, investigate why before just reporting the
disagreement. Plausible explanations include differences in study design,
sample size, sampling effort, detection probability, geographic variation,
habitat differences, species-specific ecology, seasonality, climate, human
disturbance, statistical methodology, model assumptions, analytical or
temporal scale, data quality, or publication bias. "These two studies
disagree" is an incomplete sentence — finish it with the most plausible
reason why, even if that reason has to be stated as a hypothesis rather than
a certainty.

## Weighting evidence

Not every study should count equally toward a conclusion. Weight more heavily
studies with robust methodology, adequate sample size, transparent reporting,
appropriate statistics, independent replication, strong internal and external
validity, long-term datasets, broad geographic coverage, or that are
meta-analyses/systematic reviews. Weight down studies with serious
methodological limitations identified during the critical-reading stage —
but don't silently drop them; note that they exist and why they were
down-weighted, especially if they're the only evidence on a sub-question.

## Identifying patterns

Look across the whole set of reviewed papers for recurring patterns: repeated
ecological mechanisms, consistent behavioral responses, recurring conservation
outcomes, methodological weaknesses that keep showing up, commonly reported
limitations, shared analytical approaches, emerging trends, or shifts in
research priorities over time. Explain a pattern when it's found — don't just
list that it exists.

## Knowledge gap analysis

Name knowledge gaps explicitly and classify them where possible: taxonomic,
geographic, temporal, methodological, analytical, data, scale, conservation,
policy, or management gaps. Explain why each gap actually matters
scientifically — an understudied topic isn't automatically a meaningful gap.
Distinguish between a genuine lack of studies, existing studies that are
low-quality, contradictory evidence that hasn't been reconciled, insufficient
replication, missing methodology, and merely limited geographic coverage —
these call for different next steps and shouldn't be collapsed into one
generic "more research is needed."

## Research opportunities

When appropriate, propose concrete future directions that follow logically
from the identified gaps. Avoid generic filler like "more studies are
needed" — instead specify which question remains open, which methods would
answer it, which species or regions need attention, which analytical
approaches would help, and what data is currently missing.

## Narrative writing

Write the review as connected scientific narrative, integrating multiple
studies per paragraph rather than marching through them one at a time. Prefer:

> "The majority of telemetry studies indicate that habitat selection is
> primarily driven by prey availability, although several studies in
> fragmented agricultural landscapes suggest anthropogenic disturbance can
> override prey-driven preferences."

over:

> "Author A found that habitat selection is driven by prey availability.
> Author B found that disturbance matters more in agricultural landscapes.
> Author C also studied habitat selection."

Synthesis over sequential summary, consistently.

## Completion criteria

A literature review section isn't finished until it covers: current
scientific understanding, where the literature agrees, where it disagrees,
methodological limitations across the body of work, genuine knowledge gaps,
concrete research opportunities, the overall strength of evidence, and what
remains uncertain. The end product should read like the literature-review
section of a strong review paper, not like an annotated bibliography.
